Abstract and objective (printed pp. 245–246)
--------------------------------------------
The chapter proposes low-complexity models for passive-scalar transport in
environmental applications. The solution space is reduced at successive
levels. Similarity profiles are generalized to a non-symmetric travel-time
geometry for long-range transport. Parameters are identified through data
assimilation. The approach is intended to avoid solving a PDE online and to
evaluate concentration at a point without computing a full-domain solution.
Sensitivity analysis is proposed for risk analysis and source identification.

The motivation is pesticide transport in atmospheric flows, where measurements
are incomplete and variable while the number of parameters is large. Each
level provides an inlet condition for the next and uses prior physical
knowledge to restrict the admissible solution family.

Section 2 — Reduced-order modelling (printed pp. 246–247)
---------------------------------------------------------
The authors distinguish their approach from snapshot-based projection methods:
they seek to avoid computing full-model snapshots. Instead, a simpler
parameterized map approximates the direct model, with a local validation domain
rather than a claim of uniform validity over all parameter values.

Section 2.1 — Near-field solution (printed pp. 247–248)
-------------------------------------------------------
A parameterized local velocity field and passive-scalar concentration describe
the injection device and nearby vegetation. Radial and angular profiles and
functions representing vegetation effects are fitted or informed by
experimental data and device characteristics.

A key scale-separation assumption is that injection velocity is much larger
than the motion speed of the source. The local injection flow is treated as
instantaneous and assumed not to alter the overall atmospheric circulation.
The local model is mainly used to estimate the amount of pollutant that leaves
the near-ground layer and can contribute to long-range transport. The authors
explicitly characterize these assumptions as strong.

The admissible local fields are constrained to be compatible with the direct
physics, including:
* divergence-free velocity;
* passive-scalar conservation;
* compatibility with the advection equation;
* a prescribed total scalar quantity.

The exact formulae and integration domains should be transcribed from the PDF
if they are needed in code; this note deliberately does not substitute an
approximate transcription for the source equations.

Section 2.2 — Long-range transport and non-symmetric geometry
(printed pp. 248–251)
-------------------------------------------------------------
The scalar quantity leaving the near-ground layer becomes the input to a
long-range model. The starting point is a similarity-profile representation
for a plume in a uniform flow. The chapter generalizes the representation to
a spatially varying wind by defining distance using migration time along a
wind characteristic.

The transport distance is directional. If A is upstream of B, the travel time
from A toward B is finite under the model, whereas the reverse distance is
defined as infinite. This is not a symmetric metric in the usual mathematical
sense. A transverse Euclidean distance is also used to describe spread away
from the characteristic.

The construction assumes a unique relevant characteristic and addresses
non-uniqueness by a stated projection-selection rule. This restricts its
applicability: sources, attraction points, complicated characteristic topology,
and recirculation may violate or complicate the assumptions.

For numerical construction, the authors approximate characteristics by
polynomial curves and estimate migration time along those curves. The text
notes a geometric approximation in which the target point is treated as lying
on the characteristic, then introduces a correction factor. The transverse
distance is also approximated. The authors report satisfactory behaviour for
smooth atmospheric fields in the intended application regime; this is not a
general guarantee for arbitrary velocity fields.

Ground/topography effects are introduced using a terrain representation.
The chapter notes the difficulty of obtaining sufficiently detailed terrain
and atmospheric observations for a full direct simulation.

Data assimilation and flow construction (printed pp. 250–252)
--------------------------------------------------------------
The atmospheric flow is reconstructed from sparse measurements under physical
constraints. The point is not that sparse observations uniquely determine an
arbitrary flow field, but that a restricted parameterized family is fitted to
available data. The validity of the resulting field therefore depends on the
parameterization, observation placement, and assumptions used to constrain it.

Numerical results (printed pp. 252–255)
---------------------------------------
The chapter presents illustrative configurations for a small treated field
within a much larger calculation region. It shows:
* a reconstructed divergence-free flow field using sparse wind measurements;
* a comparison between the generalized similarity solution and a direct
  transport-diffusion PDE simulation for the same wind, evaluated on the
  reference mesh nodes;
* mono-source and multi-source cases;
* the influence of terrain;
* a source-identification example using sensitivity to an observed pollution.

These are demonstrations of feasibility on selected cases, not a comprehensive
benchmark across weather regimes, terrain, source configurations, or
measurement noise. The figures contain information not recoverable from this
text note; consult the PDF for quantitative interpretation.

Conclusions and stated outlook (printed pp. 255–256)
----------------------------------------------------
The authors present low-complexity passive-scalar dispersion modelling based
on prior physical information, a travel-time-based non-symmetric geometry,
data assimilation, and sensitivity analysis. Stochastic analysis and regional
parametric risk maps via Monte Carlo simulation are described as future work
enabled by the intended low computational cost; they should not be reported as
validated results of this chapter.

Implications and limitations for J-DriftX
-----------------------------------------
1. Preserve the multi-level coupling explicitly: define the variables passed
   from the near-field model to the long-range model.
2. Treat the travel-time distance as directed and domain-dependent; do not
   silently replace it with Euclidean distance or a symmetric metric.
3. Turn physical constraints into executable checks (divergence, scalar mass,
   non-negativity, and transport compatibility), with tolerances documented.
4. Track observation coverage and parameter identifiability. Sensitivity maps
   indicate compatibility/influence; they do not by themselves establish a
   unique source or a calibrated probability distribution.
5. Keep the domain-of-validity statement close to any implementation or result.
   Smooth-flow assumptions and characteristic uniqueness are material limits.
6. Reproduce the published comparison and figures before claiming numerical
   agreement. Record mesh, boundary conditions, parameter values, error norms,
   and whether the comparison uses the same reconstructed wind.
